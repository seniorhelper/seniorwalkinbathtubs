Small Walk-In Tubs for Small Bathrooms, Short Alcoves and Shower Spaces

Small walk-in tubs prove that a tight bathroom does not rule out a safe, deep, seated bath. Compact models as short as 40 to 45 inches can fit where a standard 60-inch tub never could, including many former shower stalls, and they still give you a low step-in door, a chair-height seat and a deep soak. Here is how to find a compact walk-in tub that fits your space, what you trade for the smaller footprint, and how to combine a small tub with a shower.

Why small bathrooms need a different approach

Many older homes, condos and apartments have bathrooms that were never designed with aging in mind. The tub may be shorter than 60 inches. The bathroom may have only a stall shower. The toilet may sit so close to the tub that an outward-swinging door would hit it. In these rooms, the standard walk-in tub on a brochure simply will not fit.

That does not mean you are stuck with a tub you cannot get into, or a shower you cannot stand in. Compact walk-in tubs are built specifically for these spaces. Because you sit upright rather than lie back, a walk-in tub does not need to be long to give you a deep soak. The water rises around you while you sit on a chair-height seat, often reaching higher on your body than a longer standard tub where you recline.

Short vs. narrow: know which problem you have

Small bathrooms usually have one of two problems, and the right tub depends on which.

The space is short

If your alcove is shorter than 60 inches, or you are converting a shower stall that is 48 inches or less, look at short tubs: 40- to 52-inch models such as the Front Entry, Mobile, HD2645 or Capri. Extension panels close any small gap to the walls.

The space is narrow

If the length is fine but the width is tight, or the room is narrow so a door swing is a problem, look at narrow tubs around 26 to 27 inches wide, such as the Mobile or the 27-by-60-inch Lounger, and pay close attention to door style.

Both short and narrow

The tightest spaces call for the smallest footprints. A front-entry design can work in rooms where there is no side wall space for a door at all.

Door styles for tight spaces

In a small bathroom, the door decides whether a tub works.

  • Inward-swinging doors open into the tub, so they need no clearance in the bathroom. They are the classic choice when a toilet or vanity sits close to the tub. The trade-off is a slightly narrower entry path inside the tub.
  • Outward-swinging doors open into the room. They make entry easier, especially with a walker, but need clear floor space in front of the tub.
  • Front-entry doors sit on the short end of the tub. In a narrow bathroom where you approach the tub head-on, this can be the only layout that works.

Before choosing, measure the clear floor space in front of the tub and note where the toilet, vanity and bathroom door are. Then sketch the door swing. If an outward door would hit the toilet, choose inward or front entry.

What you trade for a smaller footprint

Compact tubs are a smart solution, but it is fair to know the trade-offs.

For many small bathrooms, the lower water volume is a real advantage: a compact tub fills faster and is easier for a standard water heater to keep hot.

Walk-in tub and shower combination in a small bathroom

In a home with one small bathroom, the walk-in tub often has to serve people who shower as well as people who soak. A compact walk-in tub can double as a shower space when it is set up correctly:

  • Handheld shower on a long hose, mounted on a slide bar so it can be used seated or standing.
  • A surround that reaches high enough to keep water off drywall, using a wall kit or extension panels.
  • A curtain rod or glass screen to keep water in the tub area.
  • Grab bars positioned for both seated bathing and standing showers.
  • A textured floor with enough flat area near the faucet to stand safely.

Many people find they prefer a seated shower in a walk-in tub over standing in a slippery stall. You get the warm water without the balance risk.

Converting a shower stall to a walk-in tub

Some of our customers have only a stall shower and want a bath again, or find standing to shower tiring and unsafe. Converting a shower stall to a compact walk-in tub is often possible, but it is more involved than replacing a tub with a tub.

  • Space. Stall showers are often 36 by 36 inches, 32 by 48 inches or 30 by 60 inches. Compare the stall dimensions carefully with compact tub footprints.
  • Drain. Shower drains are usually centered; tub drains are at one end. The drain may need to move.
  • Supply lines. A tub filler and handheld shower may need new supply connections.
  • Walls. The surround usually needs to be finished around the new tub.
  • Water heater. Showers use less hot water than baths. Check the heater before adding a tub.

Because of the extra plumbing, stall conversions can take longer than our typical one-day tub replacement. We will tell you exactly what your conversion involves before you commit. If the space is too small for any tub, a low-curb walk-in shower or roll-in shower may be better, and we supply those too.

Measuring a small space correctly

In a small bathroom, an inch matters. Measure carefully.

  1. Length of the alcove or stall, wall to wall, at the floor, middle and top. Use the smallest number.
  2. Width, from the back wall to the front edge, at the same three heights.
  3. Height to any window sill, shelf or niche above the tub area.
  4. Drain location and which end the faucet is on.
  5. Clear floor space in front of the tub for an outward door, if you want one.
  6. Doorways and hallways the tub must pass through.
  7. Water heater size in gallons.

Small walk-in tubs in condos, apartments and manufactured homes

Compact tubs are especially popular in homes where space and structure are limited.

  • Condos and apartments. Check with your association about work hours, contractor insurance and plumbing rules before scheduling. A compact tub’s lower water volume can also be easier on shared plumbing and water heaters.
  • Manufactured homes. Bathrooms are often small and floors lighter. A compact tub means less weight when filled, but the floor should still be checked.
  • Older homes. Pre-war bathrooms often have short tubs. A compact walk-in tub may fit the original space with no framing changes.

Small bathroom layouts that work

Every small bathroom is different, but most fall into a handful of patterns. Here is how a compact walk-in tub typically fits each one.

The short alcove (about 48 to 54 inches)

Common in older homes built before 60-inch tubs became standard. A 45- to 52-inch compact tub fits the space with extension panels closing the gap. If the toilet sits right beside the tub, an inward-swinging door avoids conflicts. This is often one of the simplest installations we do, because the drain is already at the end of the tub space.

The narrow bathroom with a tub across the end

Long, narrow bathrooms where you walk straight toward the tub at the far end. A standard side-door tub works if the door faces into the room with space to open. If the room is too narrow for an outward door to clear the vanity, choose an inward door, or consider a front-entry design where the space allows.

The stall-shower-only bathroom

Common in primary suites and in homes where a previous owner removed the tub. Stalls of 32 by 48 inches or larger can often take a compact tub such as a 26-by-45-inch model, with drain and supply changes. Smaller stalls may be better suited to a curbless shower with a fold-down seat.

The tight hall bath with toilet beside the tub

The toilet sits inches from the tub front. An outward door would hit it, so an inward-swinging door is the usual answer. Make sure the grab bar outside the tub does not interfere with the toilet, and consider a grab bar that serves both.

The small bathroom shared by two generations

One person needs a walk-in tub, another prefers to shower standing. A compact walk-in tub with a handheld shower on a slide bar, a tall surround and a curtain or screen serves both. Choose a model with some flat floor area near the faucet for standing showers.

Why smaller tubs are easier on your water heater

One quiet benefit of a compact tub is that it usually holds less water, which makes a hot, full bath easier to achieve with an ordinary water heater. At a typical 120°F heater setting, with cold water entering at 55°F and a bath at 102°F, about 72% of the fill comes from the water heater.

Making the rest of a small bathroom safer

In a small bathroom, every fixture is close to every other, which can be an advantage: grab bars and supports can be within reach wherever you stand. A few changes make a compact bathroom much safer.

  • A grab bar beside the toilet and one outside the tub for stepping out.
  • A comfort-height toilet, since getting on and off the toilet becomes a larger share of bathroom injuries with age, according to CDC data.
  • Slip-resistant flooring or a rubber-backed mat outside the tub.
  • Bright, even lighting and a night-light for nighttime trips.
  • A pocket door or outward-swinging bathroom door, so a person who falls cannot block the door from inside.
  • A small fold-down bench for sitting to dry off, if space allows.

A checklist for buying a compact walk-in tub

  • Measure length, width and height at three points and use the smallest.
  • Note the drain location and faucet end.
  • Decide on inward, outward or front-entry door based on the room.
  • Check seat width and interior length against the bather.
  • Check doorways and hallways for the tub’s path in.
  • Confirm your water heater capacity.
  • Decide whether you need a shower in the same space.
  • Decide whether you want jets and whether an electrical circuit is available.
  • Ask for the warranty terms in writing.

Cost of a small walk-in tub

Compact tubs are not always cheaper than standard ones, because the door, seat and safety systems cost about the same to build regardless of length. Savings usually come from simpler installation: a tub that fits the existing space avoids framing changes, and a smaller water volume may avoid a water heater upgrade. Retirement Living reports standard soaking walk-in tubs typically cost $2,500 to $5,000, and jets or larger sizes raise that. See our cost guide for the full picture.

Mistakes to avoid in small bathrooms

  • Measuring only at the floor. Walls are rarely perfectly straight. A space that is 48 inches at the floor may be 47 at the top.
  • Forgetting the door swing. An outward door that hits the toilet makes the tub hard to use every day.
  • Choosing by length alone. A short tub that is too narrow for the bather’s hips is not a good fit.
  • Ignoring the path in. Small bathrooms often have narrow doors and tight hallway turns.
  • Losing the only shower. If the bathroom must also serve a person who showers standing, plan the handheld shower and surround from the start.
  • Skipping the grab bar outside the tub. Stepping out onto the bathroom floor is a moment that needs support.

Getting in and out of a compact tub safely

A compact tub has the same safety features as a full-size walk-in tub, but the space is tighter, so technique helps.

  1. Open the door fully and hold the grab bar before stepping in.
  2. Step in with your stronger leg first, then turn and sit on the seat.
  3. Bring the other leg in, then close and latch the door.
  4. Use the handheld shower to stay warm while the tub fills.
  5. After bathing, let the tub drain completely before opening the door.
  6. Stand slowly using the grab bar, pause for a moment, then step out with your stronger leg first.

Keep a towel and robe within reach of the seat so you can start drying off before you stand.

When a tub is not the right answer

Sometimes a small bathroom is simply too small for any tub, or the bather cannot step even a few inches. In those cases, a shower solution may be safer.

  • A low-curb walk-in shower replaces a tub or stall with a shower that has a small curb, a fold-down seat and a handheld shower.
  • A curbless or roll-in shower removes the curb entirely, which suits wheelchair users and people who shuffle when they walk.
  • A tub-to-shower conversion can often use the existing tub space.

Planning a few years ahead

If mobility is likely to change, think about the next five years when choosing a compact tub. An outward or wider door is easier with a walker later. A seat at chair height is easier as knees stiffen. Grab bars mounted into studs now can support more weight later. A tub that works today and in five years saves the cost and disruption of a second project.

Frequently asked questions

What is the smallest walk-in tub available?

Compact walk-in tubs come as short as about 40 to 45 inches. Published examples include the Ella’s Bubbles Front Entry at 32 by 40 inches, and the Ella’s Bubbles Mobile and Hydro Dimensions HD2645 at about 26 by 45 inches.

Can a walk-in tub fit in a small bathroom?

Often, yes. Compact models fit many spaces where a standard tub would not, and inward-swinging or front-entry doors avoid door-swing conflicts with toilets and vanities. We check measurements before recommending a model.

Can I put a walk-in tub where my shower stall is?

In many cases, yes, if the stall is large enough for a compact tub. It usually involves moving the drain and adjusting supply lines and walls, so it can take longer than a tub-for-tub replacement.

Do small walk-in tubs still give a deep soak?

Yes. Because you sit upright on a built-in seat, even a short walk-in tub can give a deep, seated soak. Tall bathers should check interior length for leg room.

Is an inward or outward door better for a small bathroom?

Inward-swinging doors need no clearance in the room, so they suit tight bathrooms. Outward doors make entry easier but need clear floor space. Front-entry doors suit narrow rooms approached head-on.

Can I shower in a small walk-in tub?

Yes. With a handheld shower on a slide bar, a tall surround and a curtain or glass screen, a compact walk-in tub works as a tub-and-shower combination. Many people prefer showering seated.

Do compact walk-in tubs come with jets?

Many do. Air, water and dual systems are available on a number of compact models, sometimes with fewer jets than larger tubs.

Are small walk-in tubs cheaper?

Not always. The door, seat and safety systems cost about the same regardless of size. Savings usually come from easier installation and a smaller water volume that may avoid a water heater upgrade.
