Walk-In Tub Cost: What Seniors Really Pay in 2026

The walk-in tub cost most families pay lands between about $3,500 and $10,000 installed, with a national average near $6,000, according to Fixr’s 2026 data. Below we show every published range side by side, explain what pushes a price up or down, and show you how to read a walk-in tub quote so you pay for the tub you need and nothing you don’t.

The short version: what does a walk-in tub cost?

If you only read one paragraph, read this one. A walk-in tub is not a single product with a single price. It is a tub, a door, a seat, a faucet, a drain and, sometimes, a therapy system, all fitted into a bathroom that may or may not be ready for it. That is why published estimates look so different from each other.

The most useful national benchmark comes from Fixr, which updated its walk-in tub installation data in January 2026. Fixr puts most projects between $3,500 and $10,000, with a national average of $6,000. This Old House, updated in June 2026, reports about $4,600 for a standard 60-by-32-inch tub with mid-range materials, and a full range from $1,800 to more than $20,000. Retirement Living reports a wider band of roughly $4,000 to $22,100 and says most homeowners it tracked spent around $13,100.

So which number is right? All of them, for the projects they describe. The low end is a basic soaking tub dropped into an alcove that is already the right size, with good plumbing and a capable water heater. The high end is a large hydrotherapy or two-seat tub, with electrical work, plumbing changes, a new surround and repairs to a bathroom that had hidden damage. Most senior walk-in tub projects sit in between, and the rest of this page shows you exactly where yours is likely to land.

Walk-in tub prices by type

The kind of tub you choose is the biggest single factor in cost. Retirement Living publishes these ranges for the three most common categories:

Tub typePublished price rangeWhat you get
Standard soaking walk-in tub$2,500 to $5,000Low threshold, built-in seat, grab bars, deep soak; no jets
Air bath or hydrotherapy tub$5,000 to $10,000Air jets, water jets or both, usually with an in-line heater option
Bariatric walk-in tub$7,000 to $10,000Wider body, wider seat and door, more water

Fixr also notes that two-seat walk-in tubs can run from $6,000 to $20,000 depending on features, and that luxury tubs with extras such as aromatherapy, heated seats and chromotherapy can reach $30,000 installed. Those are the exception, not the rule. Most seniors do not need a luxury package to get a safe, comfortable bath.

Soaking tubs: the best value for most people

A soaking walk-in tub does the most important job, making it safe to get in and out, for the least money. There are no pumps or blowers to buy, wire or maintain. If what you want is a deep, warm bath without the climb, this is where to start. You can often add a heated backrest without moving up to a full jet system.

Hydrotherapy tubs: paying for comfort

Water jets push heated bath water through adjustable nozzles for a firm massage. Air jets push warm air through many small openings for a gentler, bubbling feel. Dual systems include both. A jet system adds the cost of the pump or blower, the plumbing inside the tub and, in most homes, a dedicated electrical circuit. See our hydrotherapy walk-in tub guide for how each system feels and who benefits most.

Bariatric, two-seat and wheelchair tubs: paying for space

Bigger tubs cost more to build, ship and heat. A bariatric tub has a wider seat and door, and a two-seat tub like Ella’s Bubbles Big4Two measures 36 by 80 inches, which is far beyond a standard alcove. Wheelchair transfer tubs such as Ella’s Transfer60 use a wide L-shaped outward door so you can slide across from a chair, and that door hardware adds cost. Compare options on our bariatric and wheelchair-accessible pages.

What goes into the installed price

When you see a single installed price, it bundles several separate costs. Knowing what they are is how you compare quotes fairly.

1. The tub itself

This is the manufactured unit: shell, frame, door, seat, faucet, drain and any therapy system you choose. Materials matter. Fixr notes that gel-coated fiberglass is the least expensive but least durable, often landing between $2,000 and $6,000, while acrylic is the most common walk-in tub material. Porcelain and cast iron last longest but can push a walk-in tub to $10,000 to $15,000 or more even without jets.

2. Removal and disposal of the old tub

Taking out a cast-iron tub is heavy work. Some installers include removal and haul-away, some charge separately. Always check.

3. Plumbing

The new tub has to connect to your supply lines and drain. If the drain is in a different spot than your old tub’s drain, or if the pipes are old and brittle, the plumber has more to do. Walk-in tubs with fast-fill faucets may also need larger supply lines to fill at their rated speed.

4. Electrical

Jets, in-line heaters and heated backrests need power. Most jetted walk-in tubs call for a dedicated GFCI-protected circuit. If your electrical panel has room and the bathroom is easy to reach, this is straightforward. If the panel is full or the wiring path is long, it costs more.

5. Walls and surround

A walk-in tub is usually taller than the tub it replaces, so the wall above it may need new panels or tile to finish cleanly. Wall kits and extension panels are the fastest and most affordable way to do this, and they are what make one-day installation possible in many bathrooms.

6. Subfloor and framing repairs

Water damage around old tubs is common. Soft subfloor has to be repaired before a filled walk-in tub, which can weigh hundreds of pounds, sits on it. This is the cost nobody can predict until the old tub comes out, which is why an honest quote lists it as a possible add, not a surprise.

7. Water heater

A walk-in tub holds more water than most standard tubs, and it has to be hot. If your heater is too small, you may need a larger tank, a tankless unit or an in-line heater on the tub. Our water heater guide and calculator helps you check this in seconds.

8. Permits

Some cities require a plumbing or electrical permit for tub replacement, especially when a new circuit is added. Permit costs vary by location.

Hidden costs most quotes leave out

In our experience, and Zachary Wennstedt’s years managing sales teams for plumbing and national remodeling companies, the painful surprises in a bathroom project almost never come from the product. They come from the things a quick in-home sales visit did not look at. Ask about each of these before you sign:

  • Doorway width. Can the tub fit through your front door, hallway and bathroom door? A tub that has to be carried through a window, or a doorway that has to be widened, is a real cost.
  • Water heater capacity. A tub that fills lukewarm is a tub you will stop using.
  • Electrical panel space. No open breaker slots can mean a panel upgrade.
  • Flooring outside the tub. Removing the old tub can expose unfinished flooring at the edge.
  • Accessories. Grab bars outside the tub, a handheld shower, a curtain rod or glass screen for showering, and a non-slip bath mat all add up.
  • Maintenance. Door seals and jet systems need occasional care. Ask what the warranty covers and what it does not.

Is a walk-in tub worth the money?

Price only means something next to the alternative. For many families, the alternative to a walk-in tub is paying someone to help with bathing, or moving to a place where help is built in. Here is what those cost nationally according to CareScout’s 2025 Cost of Care Survey:

A non-medical caregiver costs a national median of $35 an hour, about $80,080 a year at 44 hours a week. An assisted living community costs a median of $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year. By comparison, a walk-in tub is a one-time cost that, at Fixr’s $6,000 average, equals roughly one month of assisted living.

That does not mean a walk-in tub replaces care. It means that when the tub is the specific obstacle keeping someone from bathing independently, removing it is one of the most cost-effective home changes a family can make. We cover the pros and cons honestly in Are walk-in tubs worth it?

Ways to lower the cost of a walk-in tub

You do not have to buy the most expensive tub to get a safe one. These are the moves that save real money without giving up safety:

  1. Choose a soaking tub first. Jets are wonderful, but they are optional. The door, seat, grab bars and drain are not.
  2. Pick a tub that fits the existing alcove. Keeping the drain and walls where they are saves plumbing and carpentry.
  3. Use a wall kit instead of custom tile. It is faster, cheaper and easier to clean.
  4. Check your water heater before you buy. Choosing a tub your heater can fill avoids a second purchase.
  5. Look at funding programs. Medicare Advantage home-safety benefits, Medicaid waivers, VA grants and tax rules can offset part of the cost. Our Medicare, grants and tax credits guide explains each one.
  6. Consider DIY delivery if you have a trusted installer. You buy the tub from us and pay your own plumber directly.
  7. Ask about current promotions. Manufacturer programs change during the year. We will tell you what is available when you call.

How to read a walk-in tub quote

A good quote is specific. When you compare quotes from different companies, line them up item by item:

  • Exact make and model, including the tub size and door type (inward or outward swing).
  • Therapy system details: soaking, air, water or dual, and the number of jets.
  • Faucet and drain type: fast-fill faucet, single or dual drain.
  • Heated backrest or seat, if included.
  • Wall kit or surround, with color and size.
  • Removal and disposal of the old tub.
  • Plumbing and electrical scope, including whether a new circuit is included.
  • Permits, if required where you live.
  • Warranty, for the tub and for the labor, in writing.
  • Possible adds, such as subfloor repair, stated as a price range before work begins.

Be cautious with any quote that gives one lump sum with no breakdown, or that requires you to decide the same day to get a special price. A fair offer is still fair tomorrow.

Walk-in tub cost in Canada

Canadian buyers can have a walk-in tub drop-shipped anywhere in Canada for installation by a local licensed plumber. When you request a quote, ask for freight to your address plus any brokerage and duty costs so the delivered price is complete. Then keep every invoice: Canada’s federal Home Accessibility Tax Credit lets an eligible senior, or an eligible family member supporting them, claim up to $20,000 of qualifying renovation expenses per year, for a non-refundable credit of up to $3,000. Walk-in bathtubs are commonly listed as an eligible expense. Details are on our grants and tax credits page.

Operating costs: what a bath costs to run

Walk-in tub owners sometimes worry about their utility bills. The honest answer is that a walk-in tub bath does use more hot water than a quick shower, but the cost is easy to estimate with simple physics.

To fill a tub to about 102°F when your cold water enters at 55°F and your heater is set at 120°F, roughly 72% of the fill needs to be hot water. For a 60-gallon fill, that is about 43 gallons of hot water. Heating 43 gallons of water by 65 degrees takes roughly 23,300 BTU (43 gallons × 8.34 pounds per gallon × 65 degrees). That is about 6.8 kilowatt-hours of electricity, or about a quarter of a therm of natural gas, before accounting for your heater’s efficiency.

Multiply by your local utility rate and by how many baths you take each week, and you have a realistic number. For most households it is a modest line on the monthly bill, and it buys a bath that is safer and more comfortable than the one it replaces. Jets and in-line heaters add a small amount of electricity while they run.

Timing your purchase

When you buy can matter almost as much as what you buy.

  • Before a crisis, not after. Families who plan ahead get to compare models and quotes calmly. Families who wait until after a fall often need a tub within days, which limits choices.
  • Before a hospital discharge, if you can. If a loved one is in rehab after a fall or surgery, the discharge team will want to know the bathroom is safe. Start the conversation early.
  • Around the tax year. In Canada, qualifying expenses for the Home Accessibility Tax Credit are claimed for the tax year in which they are paid. In the U.S., medical expense deductions also follow the tax year. Talk with your tax professional about timing.
  • When a funding program opens. Some local grant programs accept applications only at certain times of year. Ask your Area Agency on Aging or local housing office when to apply.

What a warranty is worth when you compare prices

Two tubs with similar prices can carry very different warranties, and the warranty is part of the cost. A cheaper tub with a short or prorated warranty can cost more over ten years than a better-backed tub that costs a little more today.

When you compare, look at four things:

  • What is covered. The shell and frame are the easy part. Door seals, pumps, blowers, heaters and electronic controls are where repairs actually happen.
  • How long. “Lifetime” should mean the life of the product for the original owner, and you should know whether coverage shrinks over time.
  • Parts vs. labor. Some warranties cover a replacement part but not the cost of a technician to install it.
  • What voids it. Many warranties require proper installation and reasonable maintenance.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a walk-in tub cost on average?

Fixr’s 2026 data puts the national average for an installed walk-in tub at $6,000, with most projects costing $3,500 to $10,000. This Old House reports about $4,600 for a standard 60-by-32-inch tub. Prices rise with jets, larger sizes and bathroom repairs.

How much does it cost to install a walk-in tub?

Installation cost depends on removal of the old tub, plumbing, electrical work for jets, wall finishing and any hidden repairs. Rather than guess, we quote installation separately from the tub so you can see exactly what the labor covers.

Why are walk-in tubs so expensive compared with regular tubs?

A walk-in tub has a sealed door, a molded seat, safety features such as grab bars and anti-scald valves, often a fast-fill faucet and fast drain, and sometimes a therapy system. It is also heavier and more complex to install than a standard tub.

Is a hydrotherapy walk-in tub worth the extra cost?

It can be if you have stiff joints, sore muscles or poor circulation and you love a massage. Retirement Living reports hydrotherapy tubs typically cost $5,000 to $10,000. If safety is the main goal and budget is tight, a soaking tub delivers the key benefits for less.

Is it cheaper to buy a walk-in tub online and install it myself?

It can be, especially if you already have a trusted plumber or are a contractor. Walk-in tub installation involves plumbing, a watertight door seal and often electrical work, so most homeowners should hire licensed trades. We provide tub-only and installed prices so you can compare.

Does insurance or Medicare pay for a walk-in tub?

Original Medicare generally does not cover walk-in tubs. Some Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid waiver programs and VA grants may help, and in Canada the Home Accessibility Tax Credit may apply. Check with your plan or program directly.

Are there ongoing costs after the tub is installed?

Mostly the cost of the extra hot water and occasional maintenance. Jetted tubs should be cleaned periodically, and door seals should be kept clean and inspected. Warranty coverage for seals and motors varies by manufacturer, so read it before you buy.

Why don’t you list prices on your website?

Because a price that ignores your bathroom is not a real price. We give you a written, itemized quote after confirming your space, plumbing, electrical and water heater, and we tell you about likely extra work before you commit.
