How to reach us

There are three easy ways to get in touch, and you can use whichever feels most comfortable.

  • Call 1-888-779-2284 (BATH). The fastest way to get answers. Many of our callers are adult children calling for a parent, and many are seniors calling for themselves. Either way, you will talk with someone who knows walk-in tubs, not a general call center.
  • Email our team using the link above. Email works well if you want to send photos and measurements of the bathroom.
  • Send the form at the bottom of this page, or use our quote request page. We will call you back to answer questions and confirm what is possible at your address.

If you are hard of hearing or prefer writing, email or the form is perfectly fine. Tell us how you would like us to reply, and we will use that method.

What happens when you contact us

We keep the first conversation short, friendly and useful. There is no sales script and no pressure. Here is what to expect.

  1. We listen first. We ask who will use the tub, what is hardest about bathing right now, and whether a cane, walker or wheelchair is part of daily life.
  2. We learn about the bathroom. Tub or shower size, where the faucet and drain are, how wide the bathroom door is, and whether the bathroom is on the first or second floor.
  3. We check your address. For installation, we confirm whether one-day installation by our factory-certified installers is available for your ZIP code. For delivery, we confirm freight to your address, including in Canada.
  4. We explain the options. Usually two or three tubs that fit, with the trade-offs of each in plain language, including the downsides.
  5. We put it in writing. When you are ready, we send a written quote that separates the tub, options, and installation or freight.
  6. You decide in your own time. Take the quote to your family, compare it with others, and call us back with questions.

What to have ready before you call

You do not need anything to call us. But if you have a few details handy, we can give you a much more specific answer on the first call.

  • The length and width of the current tub or shower space.
  • Which side the faucet and drain are on as you face the tub.
  • The width of the bathroom door.
  • The size of your water heater in gallons, from the label on the tank.
  • The bather’s height, and whether they use a mobility aid.
  • Any recommendations from a doctor, physical therapist or occupational therapist.
  • Your ZIP or postal code.

Photos help even more. If you can, take a picture of the tub from the bathroom doorway, a close-up of the faucet wall, and the water heater label. Our measuring guide shows you exactly where to measure.

Walk-in tub basics before you call

If you are new to walk-in tubs, here is a five-minute primer so the conversation starts from the same page.

A walk-in tub is a bathtub with a watertight door in the side, a low threshold to step over, and a seat built in at about the height of a chair. You open the door, step in, sit down, close the door, and let the tub fill around you. When you finish, the tub drains before you open the door and stand up from the seat. The whole design removes the two hardest moves in a standard tub: climbing over a tall wall and lowering yourself to the floor.

Walk-in tubs come in several types:

TypeWho it suits
Soaking tubMost seniors who want a safe, deep, warm bath
Hydrotherapy tubPeople with stiff joints or sore muscles who want massage jets
Bariatric or two-seat tubLarger bathers, taller bathers and couples
Wheelchair transfer tubWheelchair users who slide across onto the seat
Compact tubSmall bathrooms, short alcoves and shower-stall conversions

The features that matter most are the door, the threshold height, the seat, the grab bars, the drain speed and anti-scald protection. Jets, heated backrests and lighting are comfort upgrades.

The questions we answer every day

Some questions come up on almost every call. Here are the short answers, so you know what to expect.

“How long do I sit there while it fills?” It depends on your faucet flow, water pressure and the tub size. Fast-fill faucets help. Most people run the handheld shower on their shoulders while the tub fills so they stay warm.

“Will my water heater keep up?” Often, but not always. Walk-in tubs are deep. We check your heater size before recommending a tub, and our water heater guide has a calculator you can use yourself.

“Do walk-in tub doors leak?” Not when they are installed level and the gasket is kept clean. Leaks nearly always come from installation errors, which is why we use factory-certified installers and test the seal with a full fill.

“What does it cost?” Published national figures put most installed walk-in tubs between $3,500 and $10,000, with a $6,000 average, according to Fixr. Your quote depends on the model and your bathroom. See our cost guide.

“Does Medicare pay?” Original Medicare generally does not. Some Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid waivers, VA grants and tax rules may help. See our funding guide.

“How long does installation take?” In many homes, one day once the tub arrives. See our installation guide.

Why the bathroom is where families start

When families call us, it is often because the bathroom has become the most worrying room in the house. The data supports that instinct. In the CDC’s study of bathroom injuries treated in U.S. emergency departments, the most common activities at the time of injury were bathing or showering, slipping, using the toilet and getting out of the tub or shower.

A walk-in tub directly addresses bathing and getting out of the tub. Grab bars, a comfort-height toilet and good lighting address much of the rest. We can talk through all of it on the same call.

A note for family caregivers

If you are the person who helps a parent or spouse bathe, you are carrying a lot. Bathing assistance is physically demanding and emotionally delicate, and many caregivers tell us it is the part of the day they dread most. When you call, tell us about your role too: whether you help with every bath, how much lifting is involved, and what worries you most. A tub with an outward-swinging door, grab bars on both sides and a handheld shower on a long hose can make bath time easier and safer for both of you. Sometimes the best recommendation is the one that protects the caregiver’s back as well as the bather’s balance.

After the sale: service and warranty support

Contacting us does not stop after installation or delivery. If you ever notice a drip at the door, a jet that will not start, or a drain that seems slow, call the same number. We will help you sort out whether it is a simple adjustment, a warranty matter for the manufacturer, or something covered by our 1-year labor warranty. Keep your invoice, warranty papers and the tub’s model and serial number together so we can help quickly.

How to vet any walk-in tub company

Whether you choose us or someone else, these questions separate trustworthy dealers from high-pressure ones.

  • Do they carry more than one brand? A single-brand company can only recommend its own tubs, whether they fit you or not.
  • Do they give written, itemized quotes? One lump sum makes comparison impossible.
  • Who installs the tub? Ask whether installers are trained or certified by the manufacturer.
  • What is the warranty on the tub, and on labor? Get both in writing.
  • Do they explain the downsides? Fill and drain time, hot water needs and door seal care should come up without you asking.
  • Do they pressure you to decide today? A fair price does not expire at the end of a sales visit.
  • What do independent sources say? Check their Better Business Bureau profile and reviews.
  • Will they sell you a tub without installation? Flexibility is a sign the company is focused on your needs rather than its schedule.

Talking with a parent about a walk-in tub

Adult children often tell us the hardest part is not choosing a tub. It is starting the conversation. A few approaches help.

  • Start with their goals. “You’ve said you want to stay in this house. What would make that easier?” opens a better conversation than “You need a walk-in tub.”
  • Ask what feels hard. Many parents will admit that getting out of the tub is difficult, even if they would not bring it up themselves.
  • Talk about independence. A walk-in tub means bathing privately, on their own schedule, without asking for help.
  • Offer choices, not decisions. Let them choose between two or three tubs, colors or features.
  • Bring in a neutral voice. A doctor, therapist or a specialist like us can explain options without it feeling like a family argument.
  • Be patient. Some people need a few conversations. That is normal.

When you are ready, we are glad to speak with your parent directly, with you on the line or not, whichever they prefer.

Bathroom safety beyond the tub

When you call, it is worth asking about the rest of the bathroom too. The CDC’s bathroom injury research found that the share of injuries linked to getting on and off the toilet grew with age, reaching about 37% among adults 85 and older. A few small changes, often done at the same time as a walk-in tub, cover most of the room:

  • A grab bar beside the toilet, anchored into studs.
  • A comfort-height toilet or raised seat.
  • A grab bar outside the tub for the step onto the bathroom floor.
  • Slip-resistant flooring or rubber-backed mats.
  • Brighter lighting and a motion-activated night-light.
  • Lever-style faucet handles for arthritic hands.

Our senior bathroom safety upgrades guide covers each one. We are happy to talk through any of them on the same call, even if you are not ready for a tub yet.

When bathing already feels unsafe

If someone in your home has already fallen in the bathroom, or you are worried they will, here are a few things you can do today while you plan a longer-term solution:

  • Put a rubber-backed, non-slip mat inside and outside the tub.
  • Use a sturdy tub transfer bench or shower chair so bathing can happen seated.
  • Add a handheld shower head, which many people can install without tools.
  • Keep a phone or medical alert device within reach of the bathroom.
  • Leave a night-light on in the bathroom and hallway.
  • Avoid bathing alone in the home until a safer setup is in place, if possible.

None of these replaces a walk-in tub or accessible shower, but together they reduce risk while you decide. Then call us, and we will help you plan the permanent solution at a pace that works for your family.
