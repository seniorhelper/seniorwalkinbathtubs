Wheelchair Accessible Shower Guide: Sizes, Layout and Installation

A well-planned wheelchair accessible shower lets a wheelchair user or a caregiver bathe safely without lifting over a curb or tub wall. This guide explains the three shower types defined in the 2010 ADA Standards, the key dimensions for size, threshold, grab bars, seats and controls, how drains and floors are built for a curbless entry, and what to expect during installation in a home.

Updated September 19, 2026 by Zachary Tye Wennstedt, Aging Safely Baths. 11 minute read.

The short answer

  • The 2010 ADA Standards define transfer showers (36 by 36 inches), standard roll-in showers (at least 30 by 60 inches with a 60-inch entry) and alternate roll-in showers (36 by 60 inches).
  • Roll-in showers allow a threshold of no more than 1/2 inch.
  • Horizontal grab bars are mounted 33 to 36 inches above the floor.
  • Shower seats are 17 to 19 inches high, and handheld showers use a hose at least 59 inches long.
  • Curbless showers need a floor that slopes to the drain, often with a trench drain or a recessed pan.
  • ADA rules apply to public facilities; at home they are a strong benchmark, and bigger is often better.

Who needs a wheelchair accessible shower

A wheelchair accessible shower is designed so a person can bathe without stepping over a curb or tub wall. It suits:

  • Full-time wheelchair users.
  • People who cannot lift a foot reliably, or who shuffle when walking.
  • People who need a caregiver’s hands-on help with bathing.
  • People recovering from surgery or injury who expect limited mobility for a long time.

The data shows why getting in and out matters. In the CDC’s study of bathroom injuries treated in U.S. emergency departments, 68.3% happened in or around the tub or shower, and a separate category, transferring between a wheelchair or walker and a toilet, sink, shower or tub, was counted on its own. A shower designed around the wheelchair reduces the number of risky transfers.

If the person can transfer and loves to soak, a wheelchair transfer tub is another option. See our wheelchair-accessible walk-in tub guide and our comparison of walk-in tubs vs. roll-in showers.

The three accessible shower types

The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, section 608, define three accessible shower compartments. These rules legally apply to public facilities, but they are the most useful benchmark for home design.

Transfer shower

A small, square shower with a seat at the entry. The user parks beside the opening and slides across onto the seat. It needs less space but requires a safe transfer.

Standard roll-in shower

The most common accessible shower. It is at least 30 inches deep and 60 inches wide, open along the full long side, so a shower wheelchair can roll straight in. It often replaces a standard 60-inch bathtub.

Alternate roll-in shower

A 36-by-60-inch shower with a narrower 36-inch entry at one end. It suits layouts where the full long side cannot be open.

Key dimensions and features

Clear floor space outside the shower

The ADA calls for clear floor space of at least 30 by 60 inches adjacent to the open face of a roll-in shower, so a wheelchair can approach and turn. For transfer showers, a clear space of 36 by 48 inches is used. At home, make sure the toilet or vanity does not intrude on this space.

Threshold

For roll-in showers, the threshold should be no higher than 1/2 inch, with any change in level beveled. A truly curbless entry is easiest for wheelchairs.

Grab bars

  • Mounted 33 to 36 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the gripping surface.
  • In roll-in showers with a seat, grab bars go on the back wall and the wall opposite the seat, but not above or behind the seat.
  • In roll-in showers without a seat, grab bars go on all three walls.
  • In transfer showers, grab bars run across the control wall and the back wall.
  • Grab bars must be anchored to support body weight, not just drywall.

Seat

Where a seat is provided, the ADA calls for a seat top 17 to 19 inches above the floor. Roll-in showers often use a folding seat so the space is clear for a shower wheelchair. Transfer showers use an L-shaped or rectangular seat near the entry.

Controls and handheld shower

  • A handheld shower spray unit on a hose at least 59 inches long, usable both fixed and handheld.
  • Controls reachable from the seat or wheelchair, operable with one hand, without tight grasping or twisting.
  • An on/off control on the handheld unit.

Water temperature

The ADA requires that shower spray units deliver water no hotter than 120°F. At home, an anti-scald or thermostatic valve, plus a 120°F water heater setting as recommended by the U.S. Department of Energy, protects people with reduced sensation.

Floors and drains for a curbless shower

The hardest part of building a roll-in shower in an existing home is the floor. Water must drain to the shower drain without a curb holding it back, yet the entry must be level with the bathroom floor. Common solutions:

  • Recessed shower pan. The subfloor is lowered in the shower area so the pan’s slope ends flush with the bathroom floor. This often requires framing work, easier over a basement or crawlspace than on a slab.
  • Low-profile shower base. A base with a very low, beveled threshold that sits on the existing floor.
  • Linear or trench drain. A long drain across the shower entry or back wall, which allows a single-direction slope.
  • Collapsible water dam. A flexible rubber strip that a wheelchair can roll over but that helps keep water in the shower.
  • Waterproofing. Curbless showers need careful waterproofing beyond the shower area, because some water will reach the bathroom floor.

Step-by-step: installing a roll-in shower in a home

Every project differs, but most follow these stages.

  1. Assess the space. Measure the existing tub or shower area, doorway, clear floor space and turning room. Confirm floor structure and drain location.
  2. Choose the shower type and system. Standard roll-in, alternate roll-in or transfer, and a prefabricated base or custom tile.
  3. Plan the floor and drain. Decide how the curbless entry and slope will be achieved.
  4. Remove the old tub or shower. Demolition of the existing fixture and surrounding wall material.
  5. Add blocking for grab bars and seats. Solid wood blocking in the walls is essential for secure mounting.
  6. Plumbing. Relocate or install the drain, and set the valve and handheld shower at accessible heights.
  7. Floor and base. Install the recessed pan or low-profile base, with waterproofing.
  8. Walls. Install a wall system or tile with waterproofing.
  9. Accessories. Grab bars, seat, handheld shower on a slide bar, and any water dam.
  10. Test. Run water to confirm drainage, check for leaks and verify reach ranges from a seated position.

Home vs. ADA: where to go beyond the minimum

At home, ADA minimums are a starting point. Many families go further:

  • More room. A 36- or 42-inch-deep roll-in shower gives a caregiver space to stand inside.
  • Two handheld showers or a slide bar that works for seated and standing users.
  • A bench plus a clear area, so the shower works with or without a shower wheelchair.
  • Heated floors to keep the bathroom warm for someone sitting wet.
  • Extra grab bars where the person actually reaches, based on how they move.
  • Contrasting colors between floor, walls and grab bars for people with low vision.

Paying for an accessible shower

Veterans may qualify for the VA Home Improvements and Structural Alterations grant, a lifetime benefit of up to $6,800 for service-connected conditions and $2,000 for other conditions, which commonly covers roll-in showers. Medicaid waiver programs, some Medicare Advantage plans, USDA rural repair grants and tax rules may also help. In Canada, the Home Accessibility Tax Credit lists wheel-in showers as a typical eligible expense. See our funding guide.

Planning checklist

  • Which shower type fits: transfer, standard roll-in or alternate roll-in?
  • Is there at least 30 by 60 inches of clear floor space in front of a roll-in shower?
  • Can the threshold be 1/2 inch or less, or fully curbless?
  • Is there solid blocking for grab bars and a seat?
  • Are grab bars planned at 33 to 36 inches on the correct walls?
  • Is a folding seat 17 to 19 inches high planned, if needed?
  • Is there a handheld shower on a hose at least 59 inches long?
  • Are controls reachable from the seat or wheelchair?
  • Is an anti-scald valve included?
  • Is the bathroom door wide enough, ideally 32 inches clear or more?

Common mistakes in accessible shower projects

  • A threshold that is too high. Even a small lip can stop a shower wheelchair.
  • No blocking in the walls. Grab bars and seats need solid anchoring.
  • Controls out of reach. Valves placed for a standing user are hard to reach from a seat.
  • A fixed shower head only. A handheld unit on a long hose is essential for seated bathing and caregivers.
  • Poor floor slope. Water escaping onto the bathroom floor creates a new slip hazard.
  • Ignoring the doorway. A great shower is useless if the wheelchair cannot get into the bathroom.
  • Glass doors that block access. A curtain or wide swinging panel usually works better than a narrow door.

Caregiver-friendly design

When a caregiver helps with bathing, design for two people:

  • A deeper shower, 36 to 42 inches, so the caregiver can stand inside.
  • A handheld shower the caregiver can reach without stepping into the spray.
  • A folding seat that can be raised when a shower wheelchair is used.
  • Grab bars positioned for the bather, not in the caregiver’s way.
  • Non-slip flooring outside the shower where the caregiver stands.
  • A place to set towels and toiletries within reach.

Maintenance and cleaning

Prefabricated shower systems with few seams are easiest to keep clean. Clean the drain cover regularly, check the seal around the base, and inspect grab bars and seat hardware for looseness a few times a year. Replace a collapsible water dam if it cracks or hardens.

Choosing between prefabricated and custom tile

Prefabricated systems combine a base, walls and often built-in blocking for grab bars and seats. They install faster, have fewer seams to clean and are designed to meet accessibility dimensions. They suit most tub-to-shower conversions.

Custom tile showers can fit unusual spaces and match any design, but they take longer, involve more trades and depend heavily on the installer’s waterproofing skill. Grout lines also need more cleaning.

For most families replacing a tub with a roll-in shower, a prefabricated system is the faster, more predictable choice.

Working with an occupational therapist

An occupational therapist can assess how the person moves, whether they can transfer, which side is stronger, where grab bars should go, and what seat height suits them. If you are working with the VA, a Medicaid waiver or a rehab team, ask for an OT home assessment before finalizing the shower design. Their recommendations often become part of funding paperwork, and they help avoid costly changes later.

Temporary solutions while you plan

If a permanent accessible shower will take time, a tub transfer bench, a handheld shower head, non-slip mats and a grab bar at the tub entry can make bathing safer in the meantime. These are not substitutes for an accessible shower, but they reduce risk while the project is planned and funded.

Frequently asked questions

What size does a wheelchair accessible shower need to be?

The 2010 ADA Standards set a standard roll-in shower at a minimum of 30 by 60 inches inside with a 60-inch entry, an alternate roll-in at 36 by 60 inches, and a transfer shower at 36 by 36 inches. At home, larger showers give caregivers more room.

What is the difference between a roll-in and a transfer shower?

A roll-in shower lets a person roll in on a shower wheelchair with no transfer. A transfer shower is a smaller square shower where the person slides from a wheelchair onto a built-in seat.

How high can the threshold be on a roll-in shower?

The ADA Standards limit the threshold on roll-in showers to 1/2 inch, with any level change beveled. A fully curbless entry is best for wheelchairs.

How high should grab bars be in an accessible shower?

Horizontal grab bars are mounted 33 to 36 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the gripping surface, and anchored to support body weight.

How high should a shower seat be?

The ADA Standards call for a seat top 17 to 19 inches above the floor.

Can I convert my bathtub into a roll-in shower?

Often, yes. A standard 60-inch tub alcove frequently becomes a roll-in shower. The main challenge is creating a curbless, sloped floor, which may require lowering the subfloor or using a low-profile base and trench drain.

Do ADA rules apply to my home?

ADA Standards apply to public facilities, not private homes. They are still an excellent benchmark for designing a safe, usable accessible shower at home.

Will the VA pay for a roll-in shower?

The VA HISA grant can help pay for medically necessary home modifications such as roll-in showers, with a lifetime benefit of up to $6,800 for service-connected conditions and $2,000 for other conditions.
