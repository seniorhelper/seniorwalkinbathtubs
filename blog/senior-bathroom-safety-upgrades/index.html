Senior Bathroom Safety Upgrades: What Matters Most, and What to Do First

The best senior bathroom safety upgrades target the moments when bathroom injuries actually happen: getting out of the tub or shower, slipping on wet surfaces, and getting on and off the toilet. This checklist puts every upgrade in order, from inexpensive changes you can make this weekend to bigger projects like a walk-in tub or curbless shower, with the CDC data that explains why each one matters.

Start with where injuries happen

Bathroom safety works best when it targets real risks, not guesses. The most detailed national data comes from a CDC study of bathroom injuries treated in U.S. emergency departments. Two findings shape every recommendation on this page.

First, more than two-thirds of bathroom injuries happened in or around the tub or shower. Second, the toilet became a bigger share of injuries with age. Among adults 85 and older, injuries on or near the toilet made up 51.7% of bathroom injuries.

The activities with the most injuries were bathing or showering, slipping, using the toilet and getting out of the tub or shower. Getting out of the tub or shower caused 9.8% of injuries, more than four times the 2.2% caused by getting in. So a safe bathroom plan focuses on the tub or shower exit, wet surfaces and the toilet. 1. Grab bars: the most overlooked upgrade

The CDC report cited a 2004 Home Safety Council finding that 63% of U.S. homes used bath mats or nonskid strips, but only 19% had grab bars. The CDC researchers suggested grab bars inside and outside the tub or shower, and beside the toilet, could help prevent bathroom injuries for everyone in the household. In AARP’s 2024 survey, 72% of adults 50 and older who expect to need home modifications to age in place anticipated needing grab bars.

Where to put them

  • At the tub or shower entry, a vertical bar to hold while stepping in and out.
  • On the long back wall of the tub or shower, a horizontal or angled bar.
  • Outside the tub, for the step onto the bathroom floor.
  • Beside the toilet, on the side wall, for sitting and standing.

How high

The 2010 ADA Standards call for horizontal grab bars in accessible showers to be mounted 33 to 36 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the gripping surface. Those standards apply to public facilities, but they are a helpful starting point at home. The best height is the one the person can reach comfortably, so have them try a position before the bar goes up.

How to install them

Grab bars must be anchored into wall studs or solid blocking, or with anchors rated for the purpose. A towel bar is not a grab bar, and suction-cup bars should not be relied on to support body weight. If in doubt, hire a professional.

2. The toilet: sitting and standing safely

Because toilet-related injuries rise steeply with age, the toilet deserves as much attention as the tub. The CDC noted that standing up after sitting can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure, known as postural hypotension, which is a fall risk, and that people who experience it can reduce symptoms by standing up slowly.

  • Grab bar beside the toilet. The simplest, most effective change.
  • Comfort-height toilet or raised seat. Less distance to lower and lift.
  • Toilet safety frame. A freestanding option where wall mounting is difficult.
  • Stand up slowly. Pause before walking away.

3. Non-slip surfaces

Slipping caused 17.3% of bathroom injuries in the CDC study, and many bathing injuries involve wet surfaces.

  • Use a non-slip mat or adhesive strips inside the tub or shower, or choose a tub or shower base with a textured floor.
  • Use a rubber-backed mat outside the tub, and replace mats that slide or curl.
  • Consider slip-resistant flooring if you are remodeling.
  • Wipe up water promptly after bathing.

4. Handheld shower and seated bathing

A handheld shower on a slide bar lets a person shower seated and lets a caregiver help without leaning over a tub wall. Pair it with a sturdy shower chair or tub transfer bench, and bathing can happen seated from start to finish. The ADA Standards call for accessible shower spray units on a hose at least 59 inches long, a useful benchmark at home.

5. Lighting

  • Bright, even lighting over the tub, sink and toilet.
  • Motion-activated night-lights in the bathroom and on the path from the bedroom.
  • Light switches reachable from the doorway.

6. Water temperature

Older adults and people with reduced feeling in their feet or legs are at higher risk of scalds. The U.S. Department of Energy recommends a 120°F water heater setting for most households. An anti-scald or thermostatic valve at the tub or shower adds another layer of protection.

7. Replacing the tub: the biggest single change

For many older adults, the most important upgrade is replacing a standard tub. The standard tub asks for the two riskiest movements in the bathroom: climbing over a tall wall and pushing up from the tub floor on a wet surface. No grab bar fully removes those risks.

You have three main options:

  • A walk-in tub keeps baths possible. You step through a door over a low threshold and sit on a chair-height seat. See our overview of senior walk-in tubs.
  • A low-curb walk-in shower replaces the tub with a shower that has a small curb, a seat and a handheld shower.
  • A roll-in shower removes the curb entirely for wheelchair users and people who cannot step.

Our comparison of walk-in tubs vs. roll-in showers explains which suits whom. Aging Safely Baths offers one-day walk-in tub installation in many areas, which minimizes the disruption of the biggest upgrade on this list.

8. Doorways and space for mobility aids

If a walker or wheelchair is in the picture now or soon, the bathroom has to accommodate it. The ADA Standards call for a clear door opening of at least 32 inches and use a 60-inch circle as a turning-space benchmark. At home, offset hinges can widen a doorway slightly, and a pocket or outward-swinging bathroom door makes it easier to reach someone who has fallen.

Upgrades by budget

Low cost, do it this weekend

  • Rubber-backed mats inside and outside the tub.
  • Night-lights along the path to the bathroom.
  • A handheld shower head, which often screws onto the existing shower arm.
  • A shower chair or tub transfer bench.
  • Moving everyday items within easy reach.

Moderate projects

  • Professionally installed grab bars at the tub, shower and toilet.
  • A comfort-height toilet.
  • Lever faucets and an anti-scald valve.
  • Brighter lighting fixtures.

Major projects

  • A walk-in tub, walk-in shower or roll-in shower.
  • Widening the bathroom door.
  • Slip-resistant flooring and a reconfigured layout.

A walk-through checklist

Walk through the bathroom with the person who uses it and check each item.

  • Can they step in and out of the tub or shower without climbing or twisting?
  • Is there something solid to hold when entering, exiting and standing up from the toilet?
  • Are the floors dry and slip-resistant, with mats that stay put?
  • Can they sit while bathing if they need to?
  • Can they reach the soap, shampoo and towel without bending or stretching?
  • Is the water temperature limited to a safe maximum?
  • Is the path from the bedroom well lit at night?
  • Can a caregiver reach them easily if they need help?
  • Is a phone or medical alert device within reach?

Any “no” is a place to start.

Talking with an older adult about bathroom changes

People can be sensitive about safety changes. Frame upgrades as tools for independence: a grab bar means bathing without help, and a walk-in tub means keeping baths. Involve the person in choosing colors, styles and placement. Most people are far more willing to use changes they helped choose.

Upgrades for specific conditions

Different health conditions call for different priorities.

Arthritis in the hands

Lever faucets and handles, large-button controls, a long-handled sponge, and a handheld shower with an easy on-off button reduce gripping and twisting.

Low vision

Contrasting colors between the floor, walls, tub edge and grab bars help people see edges. Bright, even lighting without glare, and night-lights on the path, reduce trips.

Dizziness or low blood pressure

Seated bathing, grab bars at every transition, and standing up slowly matter most. The CDC noted that standing after sitting can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure.

Reduced sensation in the feet

Anti-scald valves, a water heater set at 120°F and checking water with a hand before stepping in prevent burns.

Walker users

Wider openings, outward-swinging tub doors or a curbless shower, and clear space to park the walker beside the tub or shower.

Wheelchair users

A roll-in shower or a transfer tub, clear floor space, a doorway of at least 32 inches clear, and grab bars reachable from the chair. See our wheelchair-accessible shower guide.

Memory loss

Simple, familiar fixtures, clearly marked hot and cold, and a water temperature limit. Remove clutter and unnecessary items that could confuse.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using towel bars as grab bars. They are not built to hold body weight.
  • Relying on suction-cup grab bars for support.
  • Loose bath rugs without rubber backing.
  • Waiting for a fall before making changes.
  • Upgrading the tub but not the toilet, or the reverse.
  • Choosing upgrades without the user. The person who bathes should help decide placement and style.

When to call a professional

Many small upgrades are do-it-yourself projects. Call a professional for grab bars if you are unsure about wall framing, for anything involving plumbing or electrical work, and for tub or shower replacement. A walk-in tub or curbless shower involves plumbing, waterproofing and, often, electrical work that should be done by licensed trades.

A safer bathroom routine

Equipment helps, but habits matter too. A few simple routines reduce risk every day:

  • Gather everything before bathing. Towels, clothing and toiletries within reach of the seat.
  • Sit to dress and undress when possible.
  • Turn on the light before entering, especially at night.
  • Stand up slowly after bathing or using the toilet, and pause before walking.
  • Wipe up water immediately after bathing.
  • Keep a phone or medical alert device within reach of the tub and toilet.
  • Tell someone when you are taking a bath if you live with others.
  • Keep the bathroom door unlocked, or use a lock that can be opened from outside.

Planning upgrades in phases

Few families can do everything at once, and they do not need to. A practical approach is to phase the work:

  1. This week: mats, night-lights, a handheld shower head and a shower chair or transfer bench.
  2. This month: professionally installed grab bars at the tub, shower and toilet, and brighter lighting.
  3. This year: a comfort-height toilet, lever faucets, anti-scald protection and, if needed, a walk-in tub or curbless shower.

Each phase makes the bathroom safer, and the early steps protect the person while larger projects are planned.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most important bathroom safety upgrades for seniors?

Grab bars inside and outside the tub or shower and beside the toilet, non-slip surfaces, a handheld shower with a seat, better lighting and, for many people, replacing the standard tub with a walk-in tub or curbless shower.

Where should grab bars be placed in a senior’s bathroom?

At the tub or shower entry, on the back wall of the tub or shower, outside the tub for stepping out, and beside the toilet. Mount them into studs or solid blocking at a height the person can reach comfortably.

What height should grab bars be?

The 2010 ADA Standards call for horizontal shower grab bars 33 to 36 inches above the floor. At home, adjust to the person’s reach.

Why is the toilet a fall risk for older adults?

Getting on and off the toilet requires lowering and lifting the body, and standing up can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure. The CDC found toilet-related injuries rose steeply with age.

Are bath mats enough to prevent falls?

They help with slipping, but they do not solve the climb over the tub wall or standing up from the tub floor. Grab bars and, for many people, a walk-in tub or curbless shower address those risks.

What is the safest tub for seniors?

For many seniors, a walk-in tub with a low threshold, chair-height seat, grab bars, textured floor and anti-scald valve is the safest way to keep taking baths. People who cannot step at all may be safer with a roll-in shower.

How can I make a bathroom safer on a tight budget?

Start with non-slip mats, night-lights, a handheld shower head, a shower chair or transfer bench, and professionally installed grab bars. These address many of the most common risks.

Does insurance pay for bathroom safety upgrades?

Original Medicare generally does not cover grab bars or walk-in tubs. Some Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid waivers, VA grants and local programs may help.
