Walk-In Tub Water Heater Guide: Will Your Heater Keep Up?

The most common walk-in tub problem is not the tub. It is the water heater. Walk-in tubs are deep, many hold more water than a standard tub, and roughly three-quarters of a comfortable bath comes from the heater. This guide shows the simple math, gives you a free calculator, explains tank, tankless and in-line heater options, and shows how to make sure every bath is hot all the way to the top.

Why hot water matters more with a walk-in tub

A standard bathtub is shallow. You recline, and the water covers your legs and part of your body. A walk-in tub is different. You sit upright on a seat, and the water rises high around you so you can soak your shoulders and back. That depth is what makes a walk-in tub so comfortable, and it is also why it can use more water than a standard tub.

Because you sit in a walk-in tub while it fills, you also notice immediately if the water runs lukewarm near the end of the fill. There is no easy way to top it up with hot water once the heater has run low. So before choosing a walk-in tub, it is worth ten minutes to check that your water heater can keep up. The good news: the math is simple, and the fixes are straightforward.

The simple math behind a hot bath

A bath is a mix of hot water from the heater and cold water from the supply line. The share that has to come from the heater depends on three temperatures:

  • Heater setting. The U.S. Department of Energy recommends 120°F for most households, which also reduces scald risk.
  • Incoming cold water temperature. This varies with climate and season, from about 40°F in northern winters to 70°F or more in southern summers.
  • Bath temperature. Most people like a bath somewhere between 98°F and 104°F.

The formula is:

Hot water share = (bath temperature − cold temperature) ÷ (heater temperature − cold temperature)

With a 102°F bath, 55°F cold water and a 120°F heater, the hot share is (102 − 55) ÷ (120 − 55) = 47 ÷ 65, or about 72%. So nearly three-quarters of the water in a comfortable bath has to come out of the heater.

At that share, a 40-gallon fill needs about 29 gallons of hot water, a 60-gallon fill needs about 43 gallons, and an 80-gallon fill needs about 58 gallons.

How much hot water does your heater actually deliver?

Here is the part that surprises most people. A 50-gallon tank does not deliver 50 gallons of hot water. As hot water is drawn from the top of the tank, cold water enters at the bottom and gradually mixes in. After a certain point, the water coming out is no longer hot enough.

A common rule of thumb is that roughly 70% of a tank’s rated capacity comes out hot before the temperature drops noticeably. By that rule:

Tank sizeApproximate usable hot water
30 gallonsAbout 21 gallons
40 gallonsAbout 28 gallons
50 gallonsAbout 35 gallons
65 gallonsAbout 46 gallons
75 gallonsAbout 53 gallons
80 gallonsAbout 56 gallons

The more accurate number is the first-hour rating, printed on the yellow EnergyGuide label on most tank water heaters. The Department of Energy explains that the first-hour rating is the amount of hot water a heater can supply in an hour starting with a full tank. It accounts for both the stored water and how quickly the heater recovers.

Worked examples

Example 1: A compact tub and a 40-gallon tank. A 45-gallon fill at the typical settings needs about 33 gallons of hot water. A 40-gallon tank delivers roughly 28 usable gallons by the rule of thumb. That is close. In summer it may be fine; in winter, when incoming water is colder, the last few gallons may be lukewarm. Options: a slightly cooler bath, turning off other hot water use during the fill, or a modest heater upgrade.

Example 2: A standard walk-in tub and a 50-gallon tank. A 60-gallon fill needs about 43 gallons of hot water. A 50-gallon tank delivers roughly 35. That is likely to fall short, especially in winter. Options: a 65- to 80-gallon tank, a tankless heater sized for the flow, or an in-line heater on the tub to hold temperature.

Example 3: A two-seat tub and a 50-gallon tank. An 80-gallon fill needs about 58 gallons of hot water. A 50-gallon tank cannot supply that. A two-seat or extra-large tub almost always needs a larger or tankless water heater. See our bariatric and two-seat walk-in tub guide.

Winter changes everything

The temperature of the water entering your home changes with the seasons. In northern climates, incoming water can be around 40°F in winter. At that temperature, the hot share for a 102°F bath rises to (102 − 40) ÷ (120 − 40) = 62 ÷ 80, or about 78%. A 60-gallon fill then needs about 47 gallons of hot water instead of 43.

In southern climates in summer, incoming water may be 70°F or warmer, and the hot share drops to about 64%. So a water heater that seems fine in July may fall short in January. If you live somewhere with cold winters, size for winter.

Your options if your heater falls short

1. Choose a smaller-fill tub

Compact and standard walk-in tubs hold less water than two-seat and extra-long models. A tub that fits your heater avoids any upgrade. See our small walk-in tubs guide.

2. Upgrade to a larger tank

Moving from a 40- or 50-gallon tank to a 65-, 75- or 80-gallon tank is a common solution. Larger tanks need more space and, for gas models, proper venting. A plumber can tell you what fits your utility area.

3. Switch to a tankless water heater

Tankless heaters heat water on demand, so they do not run out the way a tank does. The limit is flow: a tankless heater can only heat so many gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. Walk-in tubs often have fast-fill faucets, so the heater must be sized for that flow, especially in winter when the required temperature rise is larger. Ask your plumber to size a tankless unit for your tub’s faucet flow and your winter incoming water temperature.

4. Add an in-line heater on the tub

Many walk-in tubs with jets offer an in-line heater. It does not replace a water heater, and it does not heat a tub full of cold water. It helps maintain the temperature of the bath water as it circulates through the jet system, so the bath stays warm longer. It is a helpful addition, especially for longer hydrotherapy soaks. See our hydrotherapy walk-in tub guide.

5. Adjust your routine

Small habits help: avoid running the dishwasher or laundry during the fill, run the fill before anyone else showers, and let the heater recover between baths.

6. Consider a heat pump water heater

Heat pump water heaters are an efficient option in many homes, but their recovery rate can be slower than a standard electric or gas tank. If you choose one, look carefully at the first-hour rating and consider a larger size.

Should you raise the water heater temperature?

It is tempting to turn the heater up so less of it is needed per bath. Be careful. Higher heater temperatures increase scald risk, which is a particular concern for older adults and anyone with reduced sensation. The Department of Energy recommends 120°F for most households. If a higher setting is considered, a plumber can install a thermostatic mixing valve at the heater, and walk-in tubs themselves typically include an anti-scald or thermostatic valve at the faucet. Always check bath water with your hand before sitting all the way down.

Fill time: flow matters too

Hot water volume is one part of the picture. Fill speed is the other. Because you wait in the tub while it fills, faster fills are more comfortable. Fill time depends on:

  • Faucet flow. Fast-fill faucets deliver more gallons per minute than standard tub spouts.
  • Water pressure and pipe size. Small or old supply lines limit flow.
  • Tub volume. Bigger tubs take longer to fill.

A simple estimate is tub fill divided by faucet flow. A 60-gallon fill at 10 gallons per minute takes about 6 minutes; at 5 gallons per minute, about 12. Your installer can check whether your supply lines support a fast-fill faucet’s rated flow.

Energy cost of a walk-in tub bath

Heating water takes energy, and the math is straightforward. Water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon, and it takes one BTU to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. Heating 43 gallons from 55°F to 120°F takes about 43 × 8.34 × 65, or roughly 23,300 BTU. That equals about 6.8 kilowatt-hours of electricity, or about a quarter of a therm of natural gas, before accounting for the heater’s efficiency. Multiply by your local rate and your number of baths per week for a realistic monthly cost.

Checklist before you buy a walk-in tub

  1. Find your water heater’s size and first-hour rating on the EnergyGuide label.
  2. Note whether it is gas, electric, heat pump or tankless.
  3. Ask for the tub’s fill volume with a bather seated.
  4. Use the calculator with your winter incoming water temperature.
  5. Check the faucet’s rated flow and your supply lines.
  6. Decide whether you want an in-line heater with jets.
  7. Plan any heater upgrade at the same time as the tub, so both are ready together.

Gas vs. electric tank heaters

Gas and electric tank heaters of the same size do not always deliver the same amount of hot water in an hour. Gas heaters generally recover faster, meaning they reheat incoming cold water more quickly, which gives them a higher first-hour rating than an electric tank of the same capacity. Electric tanks typically recover more slowly. That is why the first-hour rating on the EnergyGuide label is a better comparison tool than tank size alone, and why an electric household may need a larger tank to fill the same walk-in tub.

Planning an upgrade with your tub

If your water heater needs an upgrade, plan it alongside the tub:

  • Schedule both for the same week, so the first bath is a hot one.
  • Check space and venting for a larger gas tank or a tankless unit.
  • Check electrical capacity for a larger electric tank or electric tankless unit, which can require substantial power.
  • Keep the heater at 120°F and rely on the tub’s anti-scald valve for comfort and safety.
  • Ask about permits, since water heater replacements often require one.

Frequently asked questions

What size water heater do I need for a walk-in tub?

It depends on the tub’s fill volume, your climate and your bath temperature. At typical settings about 72% of the bath comes from the heater, so a 60-gallon fill needs about 43 gallons of hot water. Many homes need a 65- to 80-gallon tank or a properly sized tankless heater for larger tubs.

How much water does a walk-in tub hold?

It varies by model. Compact tubs hold less and two-seat tubs hold much more. Always ask for the fill volume with a bather seated, and compare it with your heater using our calculator.

Will a 40-gallon water heater fill a walk-in tub?

Sometimes, for a compact tub in warm weather. By the common rule of thumb, a 40-gallon tank delivers about 28 gallons of usable hot water, which may fall short for standard or larger walk-in tubs, especially in winter.

Does a tankless water heater work with a walk-in tub?

Yes, if it is sized for the tub faucet’s flow and your winter temperature rise. A tankless heater will not run out, but it can only heat a limited number of gallons per minute.

What does an in-line heater do in a walk-in tub?

It helps keep the bath water warm as it circulates through a jet system. It does not replace a water heater or heat a tub filled with cold water.

Should I turn my water heater up for a walk-in tub?

Be cautious. Higher settings increase scald risk. The Department of Energy recommends 120°F for most homes. If a higher setting is considered, a thermostatic mixing valve should be installed, and you should always check the water before sitting down.

Why is my walk-in tub bath lukewarm?

Usually because the water heater cannot supply enough hot water for the tub’s fill volume, especially in winter when incoming water is colder. Other hot water use during the fill can also be a cause.

How long does a walk-in tub take to fill?

Divide the fill volume by the faucet’s flow rate. A 60-gallon fill at 10 gallons per minute takes about 6 minutes. Water pressure and supply line size affect the real-world flow.
